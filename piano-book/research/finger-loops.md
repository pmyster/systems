# Finger Loops: Finger-Skill Exercises You Can Repeat Anywhere

Research notes, draft for discussion.

A **finger loop** is a short repeating pattern that:
- takes 30 seconds to 2 minutes,
- can be done **at the piano, on a table, on your leg, or in your head**,
- has a clear speed target and a pass/fail check,
- links to a theory idea, so fingers and understanding grow together.

Sessions are **5 or 10 minutes**. Loops are what you carry *between* sessions:
waiting for coffee, on a call, as a passenger in a car.

---

## 1. Why practising away from the piano works (and its limits)

- **Mental practice is real.** In a classic study (Pascual-Leone et al., 1995), people who
  only *imagined* playing a five-finger piano exercise showed brain changes in the
  motor cortex similar to people who physically practised. Physical practice was still
  better, but **imagined + physical beats physical alone for the same time at the keys**.
- **Tabletop practice trains the movement pattern and finger independence**, but not key
  weight, sound or exact distances. It's a **supplement**, never a replacement.
- **Many small repetitions spread across the day** use the spacing effect, plus
  overnight consolidation (see `modern-method.md` §3).

---

## 2. The three places a loop can be done

| Symbol | Where | What it trains |
|---|---|---|
| 🎹 | At the piano | Everything: movement, weight, sound, distance |
| ✋ | Table, desk, leg | Finger independence, sequence, rhythm, relaxation |
| 🧠 | In your head (eyes closed, picture the keys and fingers) | Sequence memory, note names, shapes |

Every loop in the book is marked with where it can be done.

---

## 3. Finger numbers

Both hands: **thumb = 1, index = 2, middle = 3, ring = 4, little = 5.**

---

## 4. The loop families

### Family A: Independence (one finger moves, the others stay still) ✋🎹
**A1 "Lift and tap"**: all five fingertips resting on a table. Lift ONE finger, tap twice,
put it down. Others must not move. Go 1→5, then 5→1.
- *Why:* fingers 3, 4 and 5 share tendons, so the ring finger (4) is naturally "tied"
  to its neighbours. This trains control, not raw strength.
- *Check:* film it. Pass = the resting fingers don't twitch.

**A2 "Pairs"**: alternate two fingers: 1-2-1-2, then 2-3, 3-4 (hardest), 4-5.

### Family B: Five-finger patterns, the theory link ✋🧠🎹
**B1 "Up-down"**: 1-2-3-4-5-4-3-2, repeat.
- At the piano, place it on C-D-E-F-G. Then **slide the shape** to the other 11 keys
  using the major formula (W-W-H-W). This is the ruler idea from theory, in your fingers.
- 🧠 version: eyes closed, say the note names while picturing each finger.

**B2 "Rhythm variants"**: the same notes in a different rhythm: long-short, short-long,
groups of 3. Changing the rhythm breaks autopilot and strengthens weak spots.

### Family C: Thumb crossing (needed for all scales) ✋🎹
**C1 "Thumb tunnel"**: hand on a table, fingers 2-3 resting. Slide the thumb *under*
the hand to land beyond finger 3, then back. Wrist stays level and still.
**C2 "Cross-over"**: the opposite movement: finger 3 crosses *over* the thumb.
- *Why:* scales over more than 5 notes depend on this. Train it smooth before it's needed.

### Family D: Chord-shape grabs ✋🧠🎹
**D1 "Hover and drop"**: shape a major chord (fingers 1-3-5) in the air, hover above the
table or keys, drop it down together, release, relax.
- At the piano: drop on C major, then slide to F, then G (4+3 shape every time).
- *Check:* all three fingers land at the **same instant** (one sound, not a "flam").

### Family E: Hand independence and rhythm ✋ (can be done anywhere)
**E1 "Two speeds"**: tap your legs: left hand steady beats (1, 2, 3, 4), right hand twice
as fast (1-and-2-and…). Then swap hands.
**E2 "Melody vs. chord"**: left taps once per bar, right taps a rhythm pattern.
**E3 "Three against two"** (advanced): left 2 beats, right 3 beats in the same time.
Say "**nice cup of tea**" to feel it.
- *Why:* this is the biggest piano hurdle (hands doing different things) and it can be
  trained without a piano at all.

### Family F: Speed bursts ("chunking") 🎹✋
Instead of playing a fast passage slowly from start to finish:
- Play **2 notes fast, stop**. Then 3 notes fast, stop. Then 4…
- Each burst starts from a relaxed hand.
- *Why:* fast playing is a different movement from slow playing. Bursts train the fast
  movement directly, in pieces small enough to stay clean.

### Family G: Relaxation and weight 🎹✋
**G1 "Drop and float"**: let the whole arm drop from the shoulder onto the knee or a key,
like a dead weight, then float it back up. Feel the difference between *weight* and *pushing*.
**G2 "Tension scan"**: mid-loop, check: shoulders down? wrist soft? thumb not squeezing?
- *Why (engineering):* tension = opposing muscles fighting each other. That's wasted
  force, slower movement and a strain risk. Speed comes from *removing* tension.

## 5. The tempo ladder (how every loop progresses)

1. Start on the metronome at a speed where you play it **perfectly**, e.g. 60 BPM.
2. **3 clean repetitions in a row → go up 4 BPM.**
3. One mistake → drop back 4 BPM.
4. Log your highest clean speed for the day. That's your **score** for that loop.

Progress becomes a number you can track, like a test bench.

---

## 6. Building loops into the day ("habit stacking")

Attach a loop to something you already do:

| Trigger | Loop |
|---|---|
| Waiting for coffee or kettle | A1 Lift and tap (1 min) |
| Start of a phone call (listening) | E1 Two speeds on your leg |
| Passenger in a car or bus | 🧠 B1 with note names in a new key |
| Before bed | 🧠 Replay today's piano piece in your head, slowly |

---

## 7. Safety rules (non-negotiable)

- **Pain = stop.** Never practise through pain, burning, or numbness.
- **Light touch on tables.** Fingers *tap*, they don't press. Pressing hard trains tension.
- **No more than about 5 minutes continuously on one loop**, then switch or rest.
- **Variety matters.** Obsessive, repetitive drilling of one tiny movement for hours is
  linked to overuse injury and, rarely, to a condition called musician's focal dystonia.
  Short, varied, relaxed loops are the safe way.

## 8. Loop lifecycle and the 🔁 CARRY marker (decided)

Loops live **inside the sessions**. Each loop moves through three stages:

| Stage | Where | What happens | Gate to the next stage |
|---|---|---|---|
| **1. Learn** | In session | Do the loop once or twice, following the lesson | **In-session goal** (e.g. 3 clean runs at 60 BPM) |
| **2. 🔁 CARRY** | Out of session, daily | Marker appears: *practise this between sessions until the carry goal is met* | **Carry goal**, checked at the start of a session |
| **3. ✅ LOCKED** | Review rotation | Skill is owned. It comes back occasionally in review reps so it doesn't fade | None |

### Making "the feel" testable

"It feels right" is real but vague, so each carry goal turns *feel* into checks anyone
can run. A loop is LOCKED when it passes **all four**:

1. **Cold start:** correct on the *first* try of the day, with no warm-up.
2. **Eyes closed:** you can do it without looking at your hands.
3. **Talk test:** you can do it while counting aloud or holding a simple conversation.
   (If your attention is free, the movement is automatic. This is the "feel".)
4. **Relaxed:** passes the tension scan (shoulders down, wrist soft, no squeezing),
   checked on a phone video.

Plus the loop's own number target (e.g. clean at 80 BPM on the tempo ladder).

### Rules for moving on

- A lesson that **depends on** a carried loop can't start until that loop is LOCKED.
  *Example:* scales need the thumb-crossing loop (C1) LOCKED first.
- **At most 3 loops in CARRY at once.** If you have 3, new lessons pause and sessions
  focus on locking one. This keeps the out-of-session work small enough to actually do.
- Lessons that don't depend on a carried loop can keep going, so you're never stuck
  waiting on one slow skill.

### In the book

- The 🔁 CARRY marker appears beside the loop, with its carry goal in a box.
- A **Carry tracker** page (printable) lists active loops, the date each started, and
  the four checks to tick off.
- In the PDF the marker will be our own drawn icon, not an emoji, so it prints reliably.
