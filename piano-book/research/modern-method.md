# A Modern Method: Reading, Writing and Playing Piano

Research notes, draft for discussion. Nothing here is decided yet.

---

## 1. The problem with traditional methods

Most classic methods are linear: learn the note names, then one note at a time on the
staff, then simple melodies, and real music arrives months later. The typical problems:

| Traditional habit | Why it fails |
|---|---|
| Learn symbols before sounds | Like learning to read before you can speak. The symbols mean nothing yet. |
| Read letter by letter ("Every Good Boy Does Fine") | Slow decoding. Fluent readers read *shapes and distances*, not letters. |
| Long sessions, one topic at a time | Long blocks of the same thing feel productive but stick worse than short, spaced, mixed practice (see §3). |
| Composing and improvising come "later" (usually never) | Creating is one of the strongest ways to learn, so leaving it out is a big loss. |
| Progress = turning the page | No clear pass/fail. Students move on before a skill is solid. |
| Children's songs for adults | Boring, so practice stops. |

---

## 2. An honest note about attention span

The popular claim that "people now have an 8-second attention span, shorter than a
goldfish" is a **myth**. It traces back to a marketing report with no solid study behind it.

But the conclusion is still right, for a better reason. Learning research consistently
shows that **short, focused, spaced sessions beat long ones**. So 5-minute blocks are
not dumbing down. They are how memory and motor skill actually work best.

---

## 3. The learning science we build on

These are well-established findings, not fads:

1. **Spacing effect.** The same practice time spread over several days beats one long
   session (large meta-analysis: Cepeda et al., 2006).
   → *Five 5-minute sessions across a day beat one 25-minute session.*
2. **Retrieval practice.** Testing yourself strengthens memory more than re-reading
   (Roediger & Karpicke, 2006).
   → *Every micro-lesson ends with a quick self-test, not a summary.*
3. **Interleaving / contextual interference.** Mixing related skills feels harder but
   produces better long-term skill (Shea & Morgan, 1979, motor learning).
   → *Review mixes old skills rather than repeating one drill.*
4. **Sleep consolidation.** Motor skills, such as finger sequences, improve
   *overnight* after practice (Walker et al., 2002).
   → *Daily short practice beats a weekend marathon.*
5. **Generation effect.** Things you create yourself are remembered better.
   → *Students compose tiny pieces from the first week.*
6. **Sound before symbol.** Hear it and play it first, then see how it is written.
   This is the one idea worth keeping from older approaches (Kodály, Gordon).

## 4. The core idea: the keyboard is a ruler

This is the part that will suit an engineer best.

**The half step is the unit of measurement.** It is the distance from any key to the
very next key, black or white, with nothing in between. There are 12 half steps in an
octave, and then the pattern repeats.

Once you measure in half steps, *all* of music theory becomes **shapes and distances**:

### Intervals: the distance between two notes

| Half steps | Name | Example from C | Sounds like |
|---|---|---|---|
| 0 | Unison | C → C | Same note |
| 1 | Minor 2nd | C → C♯ | Tense, "Jaws" |
| 2 | Major 2nd | C → D | A step |
| 3 | Minor 3rd | C → E♭ | Sad |
| 4 | Major 3rd | C → E | Happy |
| 5 | Perfect 4th | C → F | "Here Comes the Bride" |
| 6 | Tritone | C → F♯ | Unstable, spooky |
| 7 | Perfect 5th | C → G | Strong, open, power chord |
| 8 | Minor 6th | C → A♭ | Bittersweet |
| 9 | Major 6th | C → A | Warm |
| 10 | Minor 7th | C → B♭ | Bluesy |
| 11 | Major 7th | C → B | Dreamy, wants to resolve |
| 12 | Octave | C → C | Same note, higher |

### Scales: a specification written in half steps

| Scale | Formula (W = 2 half steps, H = 1) | Character |
|---|---|---|
| Major | W W H W W W H | Bright, "Do Re Mi" |
| Natural minor | W H W W H W W | Sad, serious |
| Major pentatonic | W W 3 W 3 | Can't-go-wrong improvising (all 5 black keys!) |
| Blues | 3 W H H 3 W | Blues and rock |

One formula works from any starting key. **Twelve keys, one spec.**

### Chords: shapes made by stacking distances

| Chord | Stack (half steps) | From C |
|---|---|---|
| Major triad | 4 + 3 | C-E-G |
| Minor triad | 3 + 4 | C-E♭-G |
| Diminished | 3 + 3 | C-E♭-G♭ |
| Augmented | 4 + 4 | C-E-G♯ |
| Dominant 7th | 4 + 3 + 3 | C-E-G-B♭ |
| Major 7th | 4 + 3 + 4 | C-E-G-B |
| Minor 7th | 3 + 4 + 3 | C-E♭-G-B♭ |

**Key insight: transposing is just sliding the shape.** A major chord is "4 then 3"
everywhere, so learn the measurement once and it works in all 12 keys.

### Chord progressions: numbers, not letters

Number the notes of a scale 1–7. Chords built on them get Roman numerals:
I ii iii IV V vi vii°. Then:

- **I–V–vi–IV** is the progression behind hundreds of pop songs.
- **I–IV–V** is the backbone of blues, rock and folk.
- **ii–V–I** is the backbone of jazz.

Learn the progression *as numbers* and you can play it in any key. Professional
session musicians work this way (the "Nashville Number System").

## 5. Improving READING

**Problem:** letter-by-letter decoding is slow.

**Our method: landmarks + distance.**
1. Learn about five **landmark notes** cold: middle C, treble G, bass F, high C, low C.
2. Read everything else as "*step* up/down" (next line/space) or "*skip*" (jump one),
   measured from the nearest landmark. This is reading distances, like the ruler idea.
3. **Piano-roll bridge.** Start with a piano-roll view (bars going left to right, height
   = pitch), which is intuitive and familiar from YouTube and apps. Then fade it toward
   real notation step by step. *Caution:* falling-notes apps that never fade out produce
   players who can't read music, so the fade-out is mandatory.
4. **Read in chunks:** recognise whole shapes (a chord, a scale fragment) at a glance,
   the way you read words rather than letters.
5. **Daily 2-minute flash drill** with spaced repetition: show a note or a shape, find it
   on the keys. Fast, scored, done.

## 6. Improving WRITING (composing)

**Problem:** traditionally never taught to beginners.

**Our method: create from week one.**
1. **Week 1:** a 4-bar black-key melody (pentatonic: impossible to sound wrong).
2. **Write it first as numbers** (scale degrees: 1 3 5 5 6 5), then in real notation.
3. **Every new concept ends with "make one"**: learn a minor chord, write a 4-bar sad
   phrase with it.
4. **Writing as checking:** if you can notate what you played, you understood it.

## 7. Improving PLAYING

**Problem:** months of exercises before anything sounds like real music.

**Our method: real music in the first week, technique built inside it.**
1. **Black-key improvisation on day 1** gives an instant musical result.
2. **Chords early:** with 4 chord shapes (I–V–vi–IV) a student can accompany real
   songs within the first couple of weeks.
3. **Two tracks in parallel:**
   - *Play-by-shape* (chords, lead sheets): fast, motivating, real songs.
   - *Play-by-reading* (notation): slower, builds the long-term skill.

   Each track feeds the other.
4. **Technique explained mechanically**, the lever-and-tension idea from Part 0.
5. **Phone as teacher:** film your hands and check against a pass/fail list
   (wrist level? fingers curved? steady with the metronome?).

## 8. The 5-minute "rep" format

Every unit is one **rep** of about 5 minutes:

| Minute | Step | Example |
|---|---|---|
| 0:00–1:00 | **See it**: one idea, one diagram | "A major chord is 4 + 3 half steps." |
| 1:00–3:30 | **Do it**: play it | Find it from C, F and G. |
| 3:30–4:30 | **Make it**: create something tiny | Make up a 2-chord loop. |
| 4:30–5:00 | **Check it**: pass/fail test | Can you find a major chord from any key in under 5 seconds? |

**A daily session = 3–6 reps** (15–30 minutes), mixed:
1 new rep + 2 review reps (spaced, older material) + 1 "play for fun" rep.

**Mastery gates replace page-turning.** You don't move on until you pass the check.
Failing a check sends you back to a specific rep, not to "the whole chapter."

---

## 9. Honest limits

- Theory can be grasped quickly. **Finger skill can't.** It still needs months and years
  of daily practice. Short reps make that practice effective, not shorter in total.
- Self-teaching misses things a teacher would catch. The video self-check helps but
  isn't a full substitute. An occasional lesson with a teacher, even once a month, is
  still worth it.
- Some of these ideas (the piano-roll fade, two parallel tracks) are design choices,
  not proven methods. We should test them on a real learner (you) and adjust.
